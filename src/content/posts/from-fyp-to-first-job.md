This is pieced together from conversations with graduates of the last four batches, now at survey and engineering consultancies, a provincial urban unit, two NGOs, a university lab and remote freelancing. None of them wanted to be named. All of them said a version of the same thing: the final year project mattered, but not in the way they expected while doing it.

## Where first jobs actually come from

Most first jobs were not advertised. They came through a supervisor's contact, an internship that turned into a contract, a senior who had left a year earlier, or a LinkedIn message answered by someone from the same institute. The open-call jobs at large organisations tend to go to people with two or three years of experience. A fresh graduate competes for internships, junior analyst positions at consultancies, project-based contracts at NGOs, and, increasingly, small remote tasks on freelancing platforms that pay in dollars.

The first salary was modest in every case. Several people said they almost did not take the offer. Everyone who stayed a year said the second job paid two to three times more, because by then they had something to show.

## What the interview looked like

A technical test was far more common than a conversation about the project. The typical pattern:

- A short practical in QGIS or ArcGIS: digitise a few parcels, georeference a scanned sheet, fix a projection problem, join a spreadsheet to a boundary layer, produce a map layout with a legend and a scale bar in thirty minutes.
- A few questions about coordinate systems, usually around UTM zones for Pakistan and why a distance measured in a geographic CRS is wrong.
- Then, if the interviewer was technical, ten minutes on the final year project. If the interviewer was a manager, two minutes.

Nobody asked about the literature review, the theoretical framework, or the accuracy figure in the abstract. One interviewer asked to see the data, not the report.

## What transferred from the project

The project itself rarely matched the job. Someone who classified crops for their FYP ended up digitising utility networks. What transferred was the work around the project:

- **Getting messy data into a usable state.** Boundaries that did not line up, images in a different CRS, a spreadsheet with district names spelled three ways. Every first job was mostly this.
- **One scripting skill, however small.** A twenty-line Python script that batch-clipped rasters was mentioned more often than any analysis. The people who could automate anything at all were given the tedious work first and the interesting work soon after.
- **Writing a method down so someone else could repeat it.** The graduates who kept a clean folder, with a readme, named files and the final maps as PDFs, could show it on a phone in the interview. The ones with a report and a thumb drive somewhere at home could not.
- **Knowing what the result could not claim.** Being able to say "the classification confuses rice and sugarcane in the south of the district, here is why" was taken as a sign of experience rather than weakness.

## What did not transfer

The sixty-page report. The accuracy percentage. The specific study area. Certificates from short online courses, unless the course came with a project link. The grade.

## If you are starting your project now

Treat it as the first entry in a portfolio rather than the last requirement of a degree.

1. Keep every script and every intermediate file in one folder from the first day, with a short readme that states the data sources, the CRS, and the steps. Put it on GitHub or a shared drive before the viva, not after.
2. Export the two or three best maps as PDFs and PNGs at the end. These are what people actually look at.
3. Write one honest paragraph on limitations and keep it ready. It is the most convincing thing you can say in an interview.
4. Practise the thirty-minute tasks above until they are boring. The job is mostly those.
5. Create your profile on this site and link the repository. Several of the people interviewed for this piece were found by the person who hired them through a profile page or a repository, not a CV.
